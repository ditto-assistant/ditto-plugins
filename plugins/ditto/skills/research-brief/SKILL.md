# Research brief

Answer a question with evidence, not recall. A good brief says what the best
sources say, where they disagree, what that means for the user, and what is
still unknown.

Use the bundled `ditto-memory` skill for connection, recall and capture rules.

## Frame the question

Restate the question and the decision it informs. Ask how deep to go and how
recent the sources must be, if that matters. Search Ditto first for earlier
research, decisions and constraints on the topic, so the brief builds on what
the user already knows instead of repeating it.

## Find and judge sources

Use web search and any connected sources the user names. Prefer primary sources:
official documentation, papers, filings, company engineering posts, datasets and
first-hand accounts. Note each source's date and who wrote it. Treat marketing
copy, anonymous summaries and AI-generated pages as weak. Look for at least one
source that disagrees with the emerging answer. Do not put private memory
details into public search queries.

## The brief

- **Answer:** two or three sentences, with your confidence.
- **Findings:** the key points, each cited with a link.
- **Disagreements:** where credible sources differ, and why.
- **Compared with what we know:** how this confirms, extends or contradicts
  earlier Ditto records, citing them.
- **Implications:** what it means for the decision.
- **Unknowns:** what could not be established, and how to find out.

Quote sparingly and summarize in your own words.

## Keep it current

Offer to save the brief to Ditto, in the team's space when the research is for a
team. For a topic that moves, offer a scheduled Ditto workflow that re-runs the
search and reports only what changed. Set either up only on the user's yes.
